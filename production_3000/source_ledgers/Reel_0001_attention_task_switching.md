# Reel_0001 Source Ledger — Switching Costs During Study

## Planned angle

When a person repeatedly switches between a study task and another demanding task, experimental task-switching research finds a performance cost compared with staying on the same task. The Reel will frame this as a short “focus block” experiment, not as a promise of better marks or a claim that all multitasking is harmful.

| Source | Source type | Supported, bounded claim | Limitation retained in script |
|---|---|---|---|
| American Psychological Association, “Multitasking: Switching costs” | Professional research explainer | Task-switching experiments measure extra time costs; studies described slower performance on switch than repeat trials, especially for more complex tasks. | The source uses varied experimental tasks, so the Reel must not state a universal percentage or turn laboratory results into a guarantee about every student. |
| Madore & Wagner, “Multicosts of Multitasking” | Peer-reviewed neuroscience forum article archived by PMC | A switch cost is reduced accuracy or speed following task shifts; control and attention systems face increased demands during switching. | Media-multitasking associations do not establish causality; the Reel will avoid claims that device use permanently damages attention. |
| Egner, “Principles of cognitive control over task focus and task switching” | *Nature Reviews Psychology* perspective, PMC author manuscript | Task focus and task-switch readiness are distinct, context-sensitive control functions; canonical task-switching studies show slower and more error-prone switch trials than repeat trials. | This is a theoretical synthesis and task findings are context dependent; the Reel will use “often” and “can” rather than deterministic wording. |

## Evidence classification

**Established experimental pattern:** Task-switch costs—slower and/or less accurate performance on switch relative to repeat trials—are documented in cognitive-control research. **Interpretation boundary:** A short period of single-task focus may reduce immediate switching demands for a study session, but it is not a clinical intervention or a guaranteed learning outcome.
