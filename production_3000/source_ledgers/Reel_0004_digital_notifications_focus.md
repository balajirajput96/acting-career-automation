# Reel_0004 — Notifications and Focus

## Classification

**Primary classification:** Established evidence from controlled laboratory research, supplemented by a university-reported two-week student experiment. **Interpretive boundary:** findings describe measured task performance or reported symptoms in the study conditions; they are not a diagnosis, a claim about every app or person, or proof that notifications cause ADHD.

| Source | Finding used | Limitation carried into narration |
|---|---|---|
| Upshaw et al., *PLOS ONE* (2022), DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0277220 | In a Navon-letter task, participants were overall slower on trials paired with smartphone-notification than control sounds. | The behavioral sample was college students; the laboratory sound/task condition cannot establish a universal rule for all notifications or daily contexts. |
| University of Virginia report of Kushlev et al. student experiment (2016) | Across two weeks, student participants reported more inattention/hyperactivity-like symptoms during a high-interruption phone condition than during a low-interruption condition. | A university news report is not a clinical diagnostic source. The report explicitly says the findings do not show smartphones cause ADHD or that notification reduction treats ADHD. |

## Script guardrails

The Reel says “participants overall responded slower” rather than “notifications ruin focus.” It labels the suggested quiet-notification / phone-distance step as a personal practical experiment, not a treatment. It avoids statistics, individual prediction, medical language, or a causal ADHD claim.
