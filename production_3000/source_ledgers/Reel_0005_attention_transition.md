# Reel_0005 Source Ledger — Attention During Task Transitions

## Editorial question

**Can a transition between two tasks be explained more accurately as a single “focus switch,” or as several interacting attention and control processes?** The Reel uses the latter as a research framework, not as a medical claim or a personal diagnosis.

| Source | Source class | What it supports in the Reel | Explicit limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petersen, S. E., & Posner, M. I. (2012). *The Attention System of the Human Brain: 20 Years After*. Annual Review of Neuroscience. PubMed PMID 22524787. | **Peer-reviewed narrative review; primary framework source.** | Attention is commonly studied through partly distinguishable alerting, orienting, and executive-control functions. | The framework is a scientific model; it does not mean daily attention has only three fixed “buttons,” nor does it diagnose an individual. |
| Schmitz, F., & Krämer, R. (2023). *Task Switching: On the Relation of Cognitive Flexibility with Cognitive Capacity*. Journal of Intelligence, PMCID PMC10140903. | **Peer-reviewed empirical study / theory-informed task-switching research.** | Task-switching work separates more than one component process, including task-set preparation and task-set inertia; switch trials can show costs relative to repeat trials in specific laboratory measures. | Laboratory task-switch results do not prove that every real-life transition is harmful, nor do they establish a universal fixed delay or productivity outcome. |
| American Psychological Association, *Multitasking: Switching Costs*. | **Professional psychology explainer; contextual, not the primary evidentiary basis.** | The page describes laboratory task-switching work as comparing switch and repeat trials, and summarizes “goal shifting” and “rule activation” as complementary executive-control stages. | This public summary is not used for numerical claims, treatment guidance, or brain-localization claims. Its examples are not a personal assessment. |

## Claim classification used in the script

| Script idea | Classification | Treatment in final wording |
|---|---|---|
| Attention is not best treated as one single mental muscle. | **Evidence-informed conceptual framing** | “Research often describes…” rather than “the brain has exactly…” |
| Alerting, orienting, and executive control are useful labels for different attention functions. | **Scientific framework** | Introduced as a research vocabulary, not as a personal scorecard. |
| A task shift can involve preparation for a new task set and residual influence from a prior one. | **Laboratory-supported mechanism** | Presented as “research tasks show…” with no claim about every person or every switch. |
| Naming the next task before beginning is a guaranteed concentration fix. | **Not asserted.** | The Reel gives only a reflective prompt: “Which job needs the next moment of attention?” No performance, clinical, or treatment promise. |

## Safety and scope

The video does **not** diagnose ADHD, cognitive impairment, stress, or any health condition; prescribe therapy or medication; assert a fixed “switch cost”; claim that multitasking is impossible in all situations; or claim that a brain region alone explains daily focus. It is an educational explanation of research language.
